### 1. Foundations and double salts

CARD 001
Q: Define coordination chemistry
A: Branch of inorganic chemistry studying coordination compounds and their properties.

CARD 002
Q: Define a coordination compound
A: A central metal ion or atom bonded through coordinate bonds to ligand molecules or ions.

CARD 003
Q: Why is coordination chemistry important?
A: Applications in industry, agriculture, medicine, pharmacy; examples include haemoglobin, chlorophyll and vitamin B12.

CARD 004
Q: Define transition elements
A: Elements with incompletely filled d or f subshells in the atom or in at least one compound (wording from chapter).

CARD 005
Q: Classify transition elements
A: Main transition d-block: three series of ten; inner transition f-block: lanthanide and actinide series of fourteen.

CARD 006
Q: State properties of transition elements
A: Variable oxidation states, unpaired electrons and paramagnetism, commonly coloured compounds, and tendency to form complexes.

CARD 007
Q: What are the two kinds of addition compounds?
A: Double salts and coordination compounds.

CARD 008
Q: Define double salt
A: An addition salt that dissociates into its constituent ions in water; the ions retain their characteristic reactions.

CARD 009
Q: Define Mohr salt and its formula
A: Iron(II) ammonium sulfate hexahydrate: FeSO4·(NH4)2SO4·6H2O.

CARD 010
Q: Write the preparation equation of Mohr salt
A: FeSO4 + (NH4)2SO4 + 6H2O → FeSO4·(NH4)2SO4·6H2O.

CARD 011
Q: What happens when ammonia is added to CuSO4?
A: A tetraamminecopper(II) complex forms: CuSO4 + 4NH3 → [Cu(NH3)4]SO4 (chapter representation).

CARD 012
Q: Distinguish double salts from coordination compounds
A: Double salt gives separate original ions in water (Mohr salt → Fe2+, NH4+, SO4^2−); a coordination compound gives intact complex ion(s) and counterions ([Cu(NH3)4]SO4 → [Cu(NH3)4]2+ + SO4^2−).

CARD 013
Q: Why is Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2 a double salt but K3[Fe(CN)6] a complex salt?
A: Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2 yields its component ions; K3[Fe(CN)6] → 3K+ + [Fe(CN)6]3−: iron and cyanide remain inside a complex ion.

CARD 014
Q: Compare mixing FeSO4 with (NH4)2SO4 and mixing CuSO4 with NH3
A: The first makes a double salt with analytically detectable Fe2+; the second makes a Cu(II)-ammine complex whose copper is coordinated within [Cu(NH3)4]2+.

CARD 015
Q: Name the two major ionic types of complexes
A: Positive complex cation and simple anion: [Co(NH3)6]Cl3; simple cation and negative complex ion: K3[Fe(CN)6].

CARD 016
Q: Give a coordination complex with a negative complex ion
A: K3[Fe(CN)6] → 3K+ + [Fe(CN)6]3−.

CARD 017
Q: Why does K[FeCl4] behave as a complex salt?
A: K[FeCl4] → K+ + [FeCl4]−; Fe3+ and the coordinated Cl− do not appear as separate free ions.

CARD 018
Q: Explain why Na2[NiCl4] is a complex salt
A: Na2[NiCl4] → 2Na+ + [NiCl4]2−; nickel and coordinated chloride remain in the complex ion.

CARD 019
Q: Explain why [Cr(NH3)6](NO3)3 is a complex salt
A: It produces [Cr(NH3)6]3+ and 3NO3− in water, not free Cr3+ ions.


### 2. Chain theory and Werner theory

CARD 020
Q: What event marks the historical beginning of coordination chemistry?
A: Preparation of CoCl3·6NH3 in 1798, according to the chapter.

CARD 021
Q: Describe the Chain Theory
A: Early model: ligands could link into chains, as carbon does, and central metal was assumed to satisfy one kind of valence.

CARD 022
Q: List hypotheses of Chain Theory
A: Cobalt forms bonds according to oxidation number; NH3 molecules bond directly or in chains; chlorine may attach directly or indirectly, explaining which chloride precipitates with AgNO3.

CARD 023
Q: Under Chain Theory, explain CoCl3·6NH3 precipitation
A: All three chloride ions precipitate readily with AgNO3.

CARD 024
Q: Under Chain Theory, explain CoCl3·5NH3 precipitation
A: Two chloride ions precipitate; one is held inside the proposed structure.

CARD 025
Q: Under Chain Theory, explain CoCl3·4NH3 precipitation
A: One chloride ion precipitates; two are proposed to be directly bonded.

CARD 026
Q: Why did Chain Theory fail for CoCl3·3NH3?
A: The proposed chain model could not correctly explain the observed absence of chloride-ion precipitation; Werner theory accounts for it.

CARD 027
Q: State Werner’s central hypothesis
A: Metals exhibit primary valence (oxidation state) and secondary valence (coordination number); secondary valences occupy definite spatial directions.

CARD 028
Q: Define primary valence
A: Metal oxidation state, satisfied by anions and generally ionizable when the anions lie outside the coordination sphere.

CARD 029
Q: Define secondary valence
A: Coordination number; number of donor atoms directly bonded to the metal; directed in space.

CARD 030
Q: Define coordination sphere
A: Bracketed region containing the central metal and its coordinated ligands.

CARD 031
Q: Define ionization sphere
A: Counterions outside the square brackets that separate in solution.

CARD 032
Q: Give Werner’s formula for CoCl3·6NH3
A: [Co(NH3)6]Cl3; primary valence 3, secondary valence 6; three free Cl− ions.

CARD 033
Q: Give Werner’s formula for CoCl3·5NH3
A: [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2; oxidation state Co = +3; coordination number = 6; two outer Cl− ions.

CARD 034
Q: Give Werner’s formula for CoCl3·4NH3
A: [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl; oxidation state Co = +3; coordination number = 6; one outer Cl− ion.

CARD 035
Q: Give Werner’s formula for CoCl3·3NH3
A: [Co(NH3)3Cl3]; oxidation state Co = +3; coordination number = 6; no counterion chloride.

CARD 036
Q: How many mol of AgCl form per mol of [Co(NH3)6]Cl3?
A: 3 mol AgCl, upon addition of excess AgNO3.

CARD 037
Q: How many mol of AgCl form per mol of [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2?
A: 2 mol AgCl, upon addition of excess AgNO3.

CARD 038
Q: How many mol of AgCl form per mol of [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl?
A: 1 mol AgCl, upon addition of excess AgNO3.

CARD 039
Q: How much AgCl precipitates from [Co(NH3)3Cl3]?
A: None from readily ionizable chloride (no free counterion chloride).

CARD 040
Q: Why do ions in a coordination sphere not precipitate as free ions?
A: They remain attached to the metal in the inner sphere during ordinary ionic dissociation.

CARD 041
Q: Write ionization and precipitation of [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2
A: [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2 → [Co(NH3)5Cl]2+ + 2Cl−; 2Ag+ + 2Cl− → 2AgCl(s).

CARD 042
Q: Which chloride ions are present in the ionization and coordination spheres of [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2?
A: Two outside the brackets are ionizable; one inside is coordinated.

CARD 043
Q: Why does IrCl3·3NH3 fail to yield an immediate chloride precipitate?
A: In the Werner-style structure [Ir(NH3)3Cl3], all three chloride ligands occupy the coordination sphere.


### 3. Bonds, ligands, oxidation state and coordination number

CARD 044
Q: Why is NH3 a Lewis base but a metal cation a Lewis acid?
A: NH3 donates its nitrogen lone pair; a metal cation accepts electron pair(s) into available orbitals.

CARD 045
Q: Define Lewis acid
A: Electron-pair acceptor; examples BF3, H+, Ag+, Cu2+, Co3+.

CARD 046
Q: Define Lewis base
A: Electron-pair donor; examples NH3 and other ligand molecules or ions.

CARD 047
Q: Define donor atom
A: An atom donating a free electron pair for a coordinate bond.

CARD 048
Q: Define acceptor atom
A: An atom with an orbital able to accept a donor electron pair.

CARD 049
Q: Distinguish NH3 and CH4 Lewis structures
A: Nitrogen in NH3 retains a lone pair available for coordination; carbon in CH4 has no corresponding lone pair.

CARD 050
Q: Define ligand
A: Molecule or ion binding to the central metal by donating electron pair(s).

CARD 051
Q: Define monodentate ligand; give examples
A: One donor atom: NH3, H2O, CN−, Cl−.

CARD 052
Q: Define bidentate ligand; give examples
A: Two donor atoms: en (ethylenediamine), oxalate (C2O4^2−), dmg as treated in chapter.

CARD 053
Q: Define chelating ligand
A: Ligand bound to the same central atom through two or more donor positions.

CARD 054
Q: Why is EDTA hexadentate?
A: EDTA can coordinate through six donor sites.

CARD 055
Q: Define central atom/ion
A: Metal atom or ion accepting the ligand electron pairs.

CARD 056
Q: Define coordination number
A: Number of attached donor atoms, not necessarily the number of separate ligands.

CARD 057
Q: Define neutral coordination complex
A: A complex carrying zero overall electrical charge and having no ionization sphere; examples [Ni(dmg)2], Ni(CO)4, [Pt(NH3)2Cl2].

CARD 058
Q: Why do some neutral coordination complexes not ionize in water?
A: They have no counterions outside the coordination sphere.

CARD 059
Q: What oxidation-state rule is used in complex calculations?
A: Sum of metal oxidation state plus all ligand charges equals total complex-ion charge.

CARD 060
Q: What are the charges of NH3, H2O, CO, CN− and Cl−?
A: NH3, H2O, CO: 0; CN−, Cl−: −1.

CARD 061
Q: What are charges of en, oxalate and dmg according to the worked ideas?
A: en = 0; oxalate = −2; dmg = −1 in [Ni(dmg)2].

CARD 062
Q: Find primary and secondary valences in [Cr(NH3)6](NO3)3
A: Cr oxidation state +3; coordination number 6.

CARD 063
Q: Find primary and secondary valences in K4[Fe(CN)6]
A: Fe oxidation state +2; coordination number 6.

CARD 064
Q: Find primary and secondary valences in [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2
A: Co oxidation state +3; coordination number 6.

CARD 065
Q: Find oxidation number of Fe in Fe(CO)5
A: 0 because five CO ligands are neutral and the complex is neutral.

CARD 066
Q: Find oxidation number of Fe in K3[Fe(CN)6]
A: +3; x − 6 = −3.

CARD 067
Q: Find oxidation number of Cr in [Cr(H2O)4Cl2]+
A: +3; x − 2 = +1.

CARD 068
Q: Find oxidation number of Fe in [Fe(C2O4)3]3−
A: +3; x − 6 = −3.

CARD 069
Q: Find oxidation number of Ni and coordination number in [Ni(dmg)2]
A: Ni = +2, coordination number = 4 (two bidentate dmg ligands).

CARD 070
Q: Find oxidation state of Ni in Ni(CO)4
A: 0; four CO ligands are neutral.

CARD 071
Q: Find oxidation state and coordination number in [Fe(CN)6]4−
A: Fe = +2, coordination number 6.

CARD 072
Q: Find oxidation state of Pt in [PtCl6]2−
A: +4; x − 6 = −2.

CARD 073
Q: Find oxidation state of Pd in [Pd(CN)4]2−
A: +2; x − 4 = −2.

CARD 074
Q: What is the distinction between valence in simple vs coordination compounds?
A: A simple compound is assigned oxidation state; in complexes both primary (oxidation state) and secondary (coordination number) valences are discussed.


### 4. EAN and metal-metal bonding

CARD 075
Q: Define effective atomic number (EAN)
A: Electron count assigned to central metal after including electrons donated by attached ligands (and metal-metal bonds where relevant).

CARD 076
Q: State the EAN rule
A: The chapter compares EAN with noble-gas atomic numbers; equality means the complex follows the EAN rule.

CARD 077
Q: Write the ordinary EAN equation
A: EAN = metal atomic number − metal oxidation number + electrons donated by ligands.

CARD 078
Q: How many electrons does one monodentate ammine or carbonyl donate?
A: Two electrons.

CARD 079
Q: How many electrons does one bidentate en ligand donate?
A: Four electrons.

CARD 080
Q: How does a single metal–metal bond count toward EAN?
A: Adds one electron to each bonded metal’s count in the simple electron-counting treatment.

CARD 081
Q: What EAN corresponds to krypton, xenon and radon?
A: Kr = 36; Xe = 54; Rn = 86.

CARD 082
Q: Calculate EAN in [Co(NH3)6]3+ (Co=27)
A: 27 − 3 + 6×2 = 36; meets Kr EAN.

CARD 083
Q: Calculate EAN in [PtCl6]2− (Pt=78)
A: 78 − 4 + 6×2 = 86; meets Rn EAN.

CARD 084
Q: Calculate EAN in [Ag(NH3)4]+ (Ag=47)
A: 47 − 1 + 4×2 = 54; meets Xe EAN.

CARD 085
Q: Calculate EAN in [Ag(NH3)2]+ (Ag=47)
A: 47 − 1 + 2×2 = 50; does not equal noble-gas atomic number.

CARD 086
Q: Calculate EAN in [Cr(NH3)6]3+ (Cr=24)
A: 24 − 3 + 12 = 33; does not satisfy noble-gas EAN.

CARD 087
Q: Calculate EAN in [Pd(NH3)6]4+ (Pd=46)
A: 46 − 4 + 12 = 54; matches Xe.

CARD 088
Q: Calculate EAN in [Fe(CN)6]3− (Fe=26)
A: 26 − 3 + 12 = 35; not Kr.

CARD 089
Q: Calculate EAN in [Fe(CN)6]4− (Fe=26)
A: 26 − 2 + 12 = 36; matches Kr.

CARD 090
Q: Calculate EAN in [Ni(en)3]2+ (Ni=28)
A: 28 − 2 + 3×4 = 38; not noble-gas number.

CARD 091
Q: Calculate EAN in [Ni(NH3)6]2+ (Ni=28)
A: 28 − 2 + 12 = 38; not noble-gas number.

CARD 092
Q: Calculate EAN in [Cu(en)3]SO4 (Cu=29)
A: Central Cu is +2: 29 − 2 + 3×4 = 39; not noble-gas number.

CARD 093
Q: Calculate EAN in [Mn(CO)5] (Mn=25), as isolated neutral fragment
A: 25 + 5×2 = 35; not noble-gas number.

CARD 094
Q: Calculate EAN per Mn atom in Mn2(CO)10 (Mn=25)
A: 25 + 5×2 + 1 from Mn–Mn bond = 36; satisfies Kr rule.

CARD 095
Q: Calculate EAN per Co in Co2(CO)8 (Co=27)
A: 27 + 4×2 + 1 from Co–Co bond = 36; satisfies Kr rule under chapter electron-counting model.

CARD 096
Q: Calculate EAN per Re atom in Re2(CO)10 (Re=75)
A: 75 + 5×2 + 1 from Re–Re bond = 86; meets Rn.

CARD 097
Q: Calculate EAN of Fe(CO)5 (Fe=26)
A: 26 + 5×2 = 36; matches Kr.

CARD 098
Q: Calculate EAN of [CoCl4]2− (Co=27)
A: 27 − 2 + 4×2 = 33; does not meet noble-gas number.


### 5. IUPAC nomenclature

CARD 099

A: Name cation before anion; within complex name ligands alphabetically, then metal with oxidation state in Roman numerals.

CARD 100

CARD 101

### 6. VBT, magnetism and shapes

CARD 120
Q: State the central VBT picture for coordination compounds
A: Ligands donate lone pairs into vacant orbitals on metal; orbital hybridization determines shape.

CARD 121
Q: Define paramagnetic and diamagnetic complexes
A: Paramagnetic complexes have one or more unpaired electrons; diamagnetic complexes have no unpaired electrons.

CARD 122
Q: State spin-only magnetic-moment formula
A: μ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is number of unpaired electrons.

CARD 123
Q: How can n be found from a known μ?
A: Solve n(n+2) = μ² using a nonnegative integer n.

CARD 124
Q: What does μ = 0 mean?
A: No unpaired electrons; diamagnetic.

CARD 125
Q: If n=1, what is μ?
A: √3 = 1.73 BM.

CARD 126
Q: If n=2, what is μ?
A: √8 = 2.83 BM.

CARD 127
Q: If n=3, what is μ?
A: √15 = 3.87 BM.

CARD 128
Q: If n=4, what is μ?
A: √24 = 4.90 BM.

CARD 129
Q: If n=5, what is μ?
A: √35 = 5.92 BM (usually approximately 5.9).

CARD 130
Q: What hybridization and shape correspond to CN=2 in chapter examples?
A: sp, linear.

CARD 131
Q: What hybridization and shape correspond to outer-orbital CN=4?
A: sp3, tetrahedral.

CARD 132
Q: What hybridization and shape correspond to inner-orbital CN=4?
A: dsp2, square planar.

CARD 133
Q: What is the common shape of coordination number 6?
A: Octahedral; d2sp3 or sp3d2 in VBT notation.

CARD 134
Q: What is geometry of [Ag(NH3)2]+?
A: Linear, sp, diamagnetic for Ag+ (4d10).

CARD 135
Q: What is geometry of [NiCl4]2−?
A: Ni2+ d8; weak-field chloride: sp3 tetrahedral, 2 unpaired electrons, paramagnetic, μ≈2.83 BM.

CARD 136
Q: What is geometry of [Ni(CN)4]2−?
A: Ni2+ d8; strong-field cyanide: dsp2 square planar, zero unpaired, diamagnetic, μ=0.

CARD 137
Q: Why are [NiCl4]2− and [Ni(CN)4]2− magnetically different?
A: Weak Cl− gives tetrahedral sp3 with two unpaired Ni2+ electrons; strong CN− produces square-planar dsp2 with paired electrons.

CARD 138
Q: Explain [PtCl4]2− according to VBT
A: Pt2+ d8, square planar dsp2, all electrons paired, diamagnetic.

CARD 139
Q: Explain [PdCl4]2− according to VBT
A: Pd2+ d8, dsp2 square planar, diamagnetic.

CARD 140
Q: Explain [Pd(CN)4]2− according to VBT
A: Pd2+ d8, dsp2 square planar, diamagnetic.

CARD 141
Q: Explain [Zn(CN)4]2− according to VBT
A: Zn2+ d10; sp3 tetrahedral, all electrons paired, diamagnetic.

CARD 142
Q: Explain [CoCl4]2− according to VBT
A: Co2+ d7; sp3 tetrahedral with three unpaired electrons, paramagnetic, μ≈3.87 BM.

CARD 143
Q: Explain [Co(H2O)4]2+ according to VBT
A: Co2+ d7, sp3 tetrahedral, three unpaired electrons, paramagnetic in the chapter treatment.

CARD 144
Q: Explain [Co(CN)4]2− according to chapter VBT case
A: Co2+ d7; refer to chapter’s orbital diagram/ligand strength discussion for exact arrangement; a four-coordinate cobalt(II) complex need not follow the Ni(II) d8 pairing pattern.

CARD 145
Q: What does a 5.9 BM moment imply for [MnL4]2− with Mn2+ and weak monodentate L?
A: n=5 for high-spin Mn2+ d5; sp3 tetrahedral geometry.

CARD 146
Q: What does μ=3.87 BM imply for [CoL4]2+ (Co2+)?
A: n=3; in the chapter’s four-coordinate case sp3, tetrahedral.

CARD 147
Q: Why is [NiCl4]2− paramagnetic while [PtCl4]2− is diamagnetic?
A: Ni2+ in a weak-field tetrahedral sp3 environment has two unpaired electrons; Pt2+ forms dsp2 square-planar with paired electrons.

CARD 148
Q: Compare [Zn(CN)4]2− with [Pd(CN)4]2−
A: Both diamagnetic; Zn(II) d10 is sp3 tetrahedral; Pd(II) d8 is dsp2 square planar.

CARD 149
Q: Compare [Ni(H2O)4]2+ and [Ni(NH3)4]2+ in textbook four-coordinate VBT discussion
A: Check the distinct orbital configurations shown in the PDF; Ni2+ is d8 and the number of unpaired electrons depends on pairing/hybridization adopted.

CARD 150
Q: State ligands classified by spectrochemical strength
A: The chapter separates weak ligands (e.g., Cl−, Br−, H2O) from strong ligands (e.g., CN−) on whether they produce pairing in relevant configurations.

CARD 151
Q: What is the structural shape of [HgI3]− in the chapter?
A: Trigonal planar (coordination number 3) in the chapter’s geometrical examples.

CARD 152
Q: What is the structural shape of [SnCl3]− in the chapter?
A: Trigonal pyramidal (coordination number 3) in the chapter’s geometrical examples.

CARD 153
Q: Name common tetrahedral coordination-number-4 examples
A: [NiCl4]2−, [CoCl4]2−, [Zn(CN)4]2−.

CARD 154
Q: Name common square-planar coordination-number-4 examples
A: [Ni(CN)4]2−, [PtCl4]2−, [Pd(CN)4]2−.
