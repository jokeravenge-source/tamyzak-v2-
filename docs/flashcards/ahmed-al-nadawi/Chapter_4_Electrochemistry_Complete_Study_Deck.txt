KEY CONSTANTS GIVEN IN THE FILE
F = 96,500 C mol^-1 e^- ; N_A = 6.02 × 10^23 mol^-1 ; R = 8.314 J mol^-1 K^-1.
Q_electric (C) = I(A) × t(s).  n(e^-) = Q_electric/F.
At 25°C, RT/F ≈ 0.026 V (natural-log form).  ln x = 2.303 log10 x.
At STP, molar gas volume ≈ 22.4 L/mol.  For other gas conditions use PV = nRT.
For an electrochemical reaction: E°cell = E°red,cathode − E°red,anode.
At 25°C: Ecell = E°cell − (0.026/n) ln Qreaction.
ΔG = −nFEcell, ΔG° = −nFE°cell, and E°cell = (0.026/n) ln Keq (at 25°C, as rounded in the PDF).

SECTION 1. OXIDATION NUMBERS AND REDOX REACTIONS [pp. 3–17, 80]
===========================================================

01 [SOURCE, p.3] Q: Define oxidation number.
A: The formal number indicating the electrical charge associated with an atom in a compound, used to track electron transfer.

02 [SOURCE, pp.3–4] Q: What is the oxidation number of an element in its free state?
A: Zero, e.g., Na, H2, O2, and P4.

03 [SOURCE, p.3] Q: What is the oxidation number of a monatomic ion?
A: Its ionic charge: K+ = +1, Fe3+ = +3, O2− = −2.

04 [SOURCE, p.3] Q: What is the oxidation number of hydrogen, and what is its exception?
A: Usually +1; in metal hydrides such as NaH, it is −1.

05 [SOURCE, p.3] Q: What is the oxidation number of oxygen, and what is its exception?
A: Usually −2; in peroxides such as H2O2, it is −1.

06 [SOURCE, p.4] Q: What are the usual oxidation numbers of Group IA, IIA, and IIIA elements in their compounds?
A: IA = +1; IIA = +2; IIIA = +3 (the values taught in the file).

07 [SOURCE, p.4] Q: What is the usual oxidation number of halogens in compounds?
A: −1 in the standard cases shown in the chapter.

08 [SOURCE, p.6] Q: Calculate the oxidation number of sulfur in H2SO4.
A: 2(+1) + S + 4(−2) = 0; S = +6.

09 [SOURCE, p.6] Q: Calculate the oxidation number of phosphorus in PO4^3−.
A: P + 4(−2) = −3; P = +5.

10 [SOURCE, pp.6–7] Q: Calculate oxidation numbers in NaIO3, H2CO3, HPO4^2−, K2Cr2O7, MnO4−, BaO, F2, KCl, Mg2+, and SO2.
A: I in NaIO3 +5; C in H2CO3 +4; P in HPO4^2− +5; Cr in K2Cr2O7 +6; Mn in MnO4− +7; Ba +2/O −2 in BaO; F2 zero; K +1/Cl −1 in KCl; Mg2+ +2; S in SO2 +4. NOTE: the HPO4 charge is faint in extraction; apply the ion's displayed charge when studying from its printed page.

11 [SOURCE, p.7] Q: Define electrochemistry.
A: The branch of chemistry studying transformations between chemical energy and electrical energy.

12 [SOURCE, pp.7–8] Q: Define oxidation–reduction (redox) reaction.
A: A reaction in which electrons transfer from one chemical species to another; oxidation and reduction occur together.

13 [SOURCE, pp.8–9] Q: Define oxidation. What happens to electrons and oxidation number?
A: Loss of electrons, with an increase in oxidation number; e.g., Na → Na+ + e−.

14 [SOURCE, pp.8–9] Q: Define reduction. What happens to electrons and oxidation number?
A: Gain of electrons, with a decrease in oxidation number; e.g., Cl2 + 2e− → 2Cl−.

15 [SOURCE, p.9] Q: Can a reduction process occur without oxidation? Explain.
A: No. Electrons lost by one species must be gained by another species.

16 [SOURCE, p.9] Q: On which side of a half-reaction are electrons written?
A: On the product side for oxidation and the reactant side for reduction.

17 [SOURCE, pp.10–11] Q: Identify oxidation and reduction and write half-reactions for Mg + Fe2+ → Mg2+ + Fe.
A: Oxidation: Mg → Mg2+ + 2e−. Reduction: Fe2+ + 2e− → Fe. Mg is oxidized; Fe2+ is reduced.

18 [SOURCE, p.10] Q: Identify oxidation and reduction for Cl2 + 2I− → 2Cl− + I2.
A: Oxidation: 2I− → I2 + 2e−. Reduction: Cl2 + 2e− → 2Cl−.

19 [SOURCE, p.10] Q: Write balanced half-reactions for 2Na + Cl2 → 2NaCl.
A: Oxidation: 2Na → 2Na+ + 2e−. Reduction: Cl2 + 2e− → 2Cl−. Equalize electron counts before addition.

20 [SOURCE, p.11] Q: Identify the oxidized/reduced species in Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu.
A: Zn is oxidized; Cu2+ is reduced. Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−; Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu.

21 [SOURCE, pp.11,16] Q: For Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2, give both half-reactions.
A: Mg → Mg2+ + 2e− and 2H+ + 2e− → H2. Cl− is a spectator ion.

22 [SOURCE, pp.11,16] Q: How many electrons pass in Cu + 2Ag+ → Cu2+ + 2Ag?
A: 2 mol of electrons per mole of the overall reaction: Cu → Cu2+ + 2e−; 2Ag+ + 2e− → 2Ag.

23 [SOURCE, p.16] Q: How many electrons are exchanged in 3Fe + 2Au3+ → 3Fe2+ + 2Au?
A: Six electrons: 3Fe → 3Fe2+ + 6e− and 2Au3+ + 6e− → 2Au.

24 [SOURCE, pp.12–13] Q: Describe oxidation of metals, reduction of metals, oxidation of nonmetals, and reduction of nonmetals as presented in the chapter.
A: Metals oxidize by forming higher positive oxidation states and reduce by forming lower oxidation states/atoms. Nonmetal anions oxidize to elemental molecules, and molecular nonmetals reduce to anions. Hydrogen is discussed as an exception to the gas trend.

25 [SOURCE, p.13] Q: Define reducing agent and state its properties.
A: A substance that causes another to be reduced while it itself is oxidized. It loses electrons and its oxidation number increases.

26 [SOURCE, pp.13,16] Q: Define oxidizing agent and state its properties.
A: A substance that causes another to be oxidized while it itself is reduced. It gains electrons and its oxidation number decreases.

27 [SOURCE, p.13] Q: Identify oxidizing and reducing agents for Cu + 4HNO3 → Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO2 + 2H2O.
A: Cu is the reducing agent (0 → +2); nitrate nitrogen in HNO3 is reduced (+5 → +4 in NO2), so HNO3 is the oxidizing reactant.

28 [SOURCE, p.14; question 4-15] Q: Identify oxidizing and reducing agents in AgNO2 + Cl2 + 2KOH → AgNO3 + 2KCl + H2O.
A: Cl2 is the oxidizing agent (0 → −1); AgNO2 is the reducing agent because N increases from +3 to +5.

29 [SOURCE, p.14] Q: Identify the agents in Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu.
A: Zn is the reducing agent; Cu2+ (in CuSO4) is the oxidizing agent.

30 [SOURCE, p.14] Q: Identify the agents in Cu + 2AgNO3 → Cu(NO3)2 + 2Ag.
A: Cu is the reducing agent; Ag+ (in AgNO3) is the oxidizing agent.

31 [SOURCE, pp.15–16] Q: Identify the agents in 2C + O2 → 2CO.
A: C is the reducing agent (0 → +2); O2 is the oxidizing agent (0 → −2).

32 [SOURCE, p.15] Q: Identify the agents in Mg + Fe2+ → Mg2+ + Fe.
A: Mg is the reducing agent; Fe2+ is the oxidizing agent.

33 [SOURCE, pp.15–16] Q: Identify the agents in Mg + Cl2 → MgCl2.
A: Mg is the reducing agent; Cl2 is the oxidizing agent.

34 [SOURCE, p.15; question 4-23] Q: Which are redox? (1) Al2O3 + HCl, (2) Na + Cl2, (3) KClO3 decomposes into KCl + O2, (4) SiBr4 hydrolysis, (5) Zn + CuSO4.
A: (2), (3), and (5). Oxidation states change in these reactions. The other listed reactions do not have net oxidation-state change.

35 [SOURCE, p.16] Q: Identify the oxidizing and reducing agents in 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O.
A: H2 is the reducing agent; O2 is the oxidizing agent.

36 [SOURCE, p.16] Q: Define oxidation potential.
A: The tendency of a substance to lose electrons.

37 [SOURCE, pp.16–17] Q: Give three characteristic facts about a reducing agent.
A: It is itself oxidized, it loses electrons, and its oxidation number increases.

202 [SOURCE, pp.5–6] Q: What must the sum of all oxidation numbers be in a neutral compound?
A: Zero. Example: in H2SO4, 2(+1) + S + 4(−2) = 0, so S = +6.

203 [SOURCE, pp.5–6] Q: What must the sum of all oxidation numbers equal in a polyatomic ion?
A: The net ionic charge. Example: in PO4^3−, P + 4(−2) = −3, so P = +5.

SECTION 2. GALVANIC CELLS, DANIELL CELL, AND SALT BRIDGE [pp.18–24]
===========================================================

38 [SOURCE, p.18] Q: Define an electrochemical cell and name its two main classes.
A: A system containing electrodes and an electrolyte where oxidation and reduction occur; classes: galvanic/voltaic cells and electrolytic cells.

39 [SOURCE, p.18] Q: At which electrode does oxidation occur? At which does reduction occur?
A: Oxidation occurs at the anode; reduction occurs at the cathode, in either kind of cell.

40 [SOURCE, p.20] Q: Define a galvanic (voltaic) cell.
A: A cell that converts energy from a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.

41 [SOURCE, p.19] Q: What happens when a zinc rod is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution?
A: Zinc dissolves as Zn2+ (oxidation); copper metal deposits as a brownish layer (Cu2+ reduction); the solution's blue color fades as Cu2+ is consumed. Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu.

42 [SOURCE, p.20] Q: When does this spontaneous Zn/Cu2+ reaction stop?
A: When the reactive zinc or available copper ions are exhausted, as described in the chapter.

43 [SOURCE, p.20] Q: State the four main components of a Daniell cell.
A: Zinc anode in ZnSO4, copper cathode in CuSO4, connecting external wire, and salt bridge.

44 [SOURCE, pp.20–21] Q: Write the two Daniell cell half-reactions and overall reaction.
A: Anode: Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−. Cathode: Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu. Overall: Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu.

45 [SOURCE, p.21] Q: Compare the physical changes at zinc and copper electrodes in Daniell cell.
A: Zinc rod becomes thinner and its ion concentration rises; copper rod becomes thicker as Cu2+ concentration declines. Electrons leave zinc and reach copper through the wire.

46 [SOURCE, p.21] Q: Why keep the Daniell cell half-cells in separate vessels?
A: To force electrons to travel through the external circuit instead of allowing Cu2+ to react directly on zinc.

47 [SOURCE, p.21] Q: Why were zinc and copper selected for the Daniell cell in the chapter?
A: They provide a useful potential difference (about 1.10 V under the supplied standard data).

48 [SOURCE, pp.22–23] Q: Define a salt bridge and name salts used to fill it.
A: A U-shaped conducting electrolyte connector, commonly containing inert electrolyte such as KNO3, KCl, or K2SO4 (and agar medium in the discussion).

49 [SOURCE, pp.22–23] Q: State the functions of the salt bridge.
A: Completes the internal ionic circuit, prevents charge buildup, and maintains electroneutrality in both half-cells.

50 [SOURCE, pp.21–23] Q: In a KNO3 salt bridge connecting Cu/Ag half-cells, where do K+ and NO3− travel?
A: K+ migrates toward the cathode; NO3− migrates toward the anode.

51 [SOURCE, p.23] Q: What is the role of the external wire?
A: It carries electrons from the anode to the cathode, completing the external circuit.

52 [SOURCE, p.23] Q: For Cu + 2Ag+ → Cu2+ + 2Ag, specify both half-reactions and electron flow.
A: Cu → Cu2+ + 2e− (anode); 2Ag+ + 2e− → 2Ag (cathode). Electrons flow Cu → Ag through the wire; anions migrate to the anode and cations to the cathode through the salt bridge.

53 [SOURCE, pp.24,30] Q: Define cell potential / EMF and give its unit.
A: Potential difference between the cell's electrodes, measured in volts (V), and often called electromotive force, emf.

54 [SOURCE, p.24] Q: List factors on which cell potential depends.
A: Electrode/ion properties, ion concentrations, temperature, and electrode reactions.

55 [SOURCE, p.30] Q: Give the signs of ΔG and Ecell in a spontaneous galvanic reaction.
A: ΔG < 0 and Ecell > 0.

SECTION 3. ELECTRODE POTENTIAL, SHE, CELL NOTATION [pp.24–58]
===========================================================

56 [SOURCE, pp.24–25] Q: Define reduction potential and oxidation potential.
A: Reduction potential reflects tendency to gain electrons; oxidation potential reflects tendency to lose electrons.

57 [SOURCE, p.25] Q: Define a reference electrode.
A: An electrode with assigned/known potential used to compare other electrode potentials.

58 [SOURCE, pp.25–26,29] Q: Describe standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) construction and standard conditions.
A: A platinum/black-Pt electrode in a 1 M H+ solution with H2 gas at 1 atm and 25°C; E°(2H+/H2) = 0.00 V.

59 [SOURCE, pp.25,29] Q: Why is platinum used in SHE?
A: It is inert under the described conditions, provides a surface for H2 reactions, and conducts electrons.

60 [SOURCE, p.26] Q: Write SHE half-reactions when acting as anode and cathode.
A: As anode: H2 → 2H+ + 2e−. As cathode: 2H+ + 2e− → H2. Both have assigned standard potential 0 V.

61 [SOURCE, pp.26–29] Q: What is the importance of SHE?
A: It is the zero-potential reference for measuring relative standard electrode potentials.

62 [SOURCE, p.26] Q: Why cannot a single standard electrode potential be measured by itself?
A: An oxidation half-reaction cannot occur independently of a reduction half-reaction; a reference electrode is needed.

63 [SOURCE, p.27] Q: How does pH change when a hydrogen electrode operates as anode in the described setup?
A: H2 oxidizes to H+, so [H+] rises and pH decreases.

64 [SOURCE, pp.27,31] Q: Which of two species with different E°reduction has stronger oxidizing tendency?
A: The one with the higher (more positive) standard reduction potential.

65 [SOURCE, pp.31–32] Q: How do you choose anode and cathode using standard reduction potentials?
A: The lower E°reduction species is used as oxidation/anode; the higher E°reduction species is reduction/cathode for the spontaneous cell.

66 [SOURCE, pp.31–32] Q: What is the standard cell potential formula?
A: E°cell = E°reduction(cathode) − E°reduction(anode), or E°oxidation(anode) + E°reduction(cathode).

67 [SOURCE, pp.31–32,51] Q: Why are E° values NOT multiplied by stoichiometric coefficients?
A: Electrode potential is an intensive property; balance electrons in half-reactions, not the electrode potentials.

68 [SOURCE, p.32] Q: What does positive or negative E°cell indicate?
A: Positive: forward reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions. Negative: forward reaction is nonspontaneous under standard conditions.

69 [SOURCE, p.33] Q: Find Daniell cell standard potential if E°Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V and E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V.
A: E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V.

70 [SOURCE, p.33; question 4-4] Q: A standard hydrogen anode and copper cathode form a cell with E°cell = +0.337 V. Find copper's E°reduction.
A: +0.337 V (the SHE reference potential is zero).

71 [SOURCE, p.34; question 4-5] Q: Find E°cell for Cd2+/Cd = −0.40 V and Cr2+/Cr = −0.74 V.
A: Cd is cathode and Cr anode; E°cell = −0.40 − (−0.74) = +0.34 V. [The file inconsistently labels chromium ion charge elsewhere; follow the given reaction data.]

72 [SOURCE, p.34] Q: What is E°cell for standard Al/Al3+ and Cu2+/Cu, given −1.66 V and +0.34 V?
A: 0.34 − (−1.66) = +2.00 V; spontaneous in the Al oxidation / Cu2+ reduction direction.

73 [SOURCE, p.35] Q: In Daniell cell E°cell = 1.10 V and E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V. Find Cu reduction and Cu oxidation potentials.
A: E°Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V, and the reverse oxidation potential Cu/Cu2+ = −0.34 V.

74 [SOURCE, p.35] Q: Does Ni + Co2+ → Ni2+ + Co occur spontaneously if E°Ni2+/Ni = −0.25 and E°Co2+/Co = −0.28 V?
A: No. E°cell = (−0.28) − (−0.25) = −0.03 V.

75 [SOURCE, pp.36–38] Q: List three electrode types described in the chapter.
A: Metal/metal-ion electrodes, gas/ion electrodes with an inert conductor (usually Pt), and redox electrodes with two oxidation states in solution and an inert Pt/graphite conductor.

76 [SOURCE, pp.36,50] Q: Write Daniell cell line notation under standard conditions.
A: Zn(s) | Zn2+(1 M) || Cu2+(1 M) | Cu(s).

77 [SOURCE, pp.37–38] Q: Explain the symbols '|' and '||' in cell notation.
A: '|' separates phases/interfaces; '||' represents the salt bridge/liquid junction. Conventionally write anode to the left and cathode to the right.

78 [SOURCE, pp.37–38] Q: Why is Pt included in gas and soluble redox electrode notation?
A: It provides an inert conducting surface when no solid conducting reactant is present.

79 [SOURCE, pp.37–38] Q: Express Cl2 + 2Ag → 2Cl− + 2Ag+ by standard cell notation.
A: Ag(s) | Ag+(1 M) || Cl−(1 M) | Cl2(1 atm) | Pt.

80 [SOURCE, p.38] Q: Express Zn + 2H+ → Zn2+ + H2 by standard cell notation.
A: Zn(s) | Zn2+(1 M) || H+(1 M) | H2(1 atm) | Pt.

81 [SOURCE, p.38] Q: In Zn + 2Fe3+ → Zn2+ + 2Fe2+ with E°cell = 1.53 V and E°Fe3+/Fe2+ = +0.77 V, find E°Zn2+/Zn and line notation.
A: E°oxidation(Zn) = 1.53 − 0.77 = +0.76 V, so E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V. Zn | Zn2+ || Fe3+,Fe2+ | Pt (standard concentrations).

82 [SOURCE, p.39] Q: Is 3Fe + 2Au3+ → 3Fe2+ + 2Au spontaneous if E°Fe2+/Fe = −0.44 V, E°Au3+/Au = +1.50 V?
A: Yes. E°cell = 1.50 − (−0.44) = +1.94 V. Notation: Fe | Fe2+ || Au3+ | Au.

83 [SOURCE, p.40; question 4-13] Q: Determine spontaneity of Pt | Fe2+,Fe3+ || Br− | Br2 | Pt, using E°Br2/Br− = 1.07 V and E°Fe3+/Fe2+ = 0.77 V.
A: Fe2+ oxidizes and Br2 reduces; E°cell = 1.07 − 0.77 = +0.30 V, spontaneous in that direction.

84 [SOURCE, pp.40–41; question 4-28] Q: In Al | Al3+ || Cd2+ | Cd, E°cell = 1.26 V and E°Cd2+/Cd = −0.40 V. Find E°Al3+/Al.
A: E°Al3+/Al = −1.66 V. E°oxidation(Al) = +1.66 V.

85 [SOURCE, p.41] Q: Given the same Al/Cd cell, E°cell = 1.26 V and E°oxidation(Al) = +1.66 V, find Cd potentials.
A: E°reduction(Cd2+/Cd) = −0.40 V, E°oxidation(Cd/Cd2+) = +0.40 V.

86 [SOURCE, pp.41–42] Q: Which two electrodes give maximum E°cell from Al3+/Al −1.66, Zn2+/Zn −0.76, F2/F− +2.87, Pb2+/Pb −0.13 V?
A: Aluminum anode and fluorine cathode. E°cell = 2.87 − (−1.66) = +4.53 V; Al | Al3+ || F− | F2 | Pt.

87 [SOURCE, pp.42,52] Q: Can NaCl solution be kept in a copper vessel according to the standard-potential test used in the chapter? E°Na+/Na = −2.70, E°Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V.
A: The tested Cu + 2Na+ → Cu2+ + 2Na has E°cell = −3.04 V, so the chapter calls it nonspontaneous and suitable to keep.

88 [SOURCE, p.43; question printed '17-4'] Q: Can CuSO4 solution be kept in a nickel vessel? E°Ni2+/Ni = −0.24 V, E°Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V.
A: No; Ni + Cu2+ → Ni2+ + Cu, E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.24) = +0.58 V, spontaneous. Other pages use −0.25 V, yielding +0.59 V; values differ in the file.

89 [SOURCE, pp.43–44] Q: Can Co(NO3)2 solution be kept in zinc or copper, given E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76, E°Cu2+/Cu = +0.34, E°Co2+/Co = −0.28 V?
A: In zinc: E°cell = (−0.28) − (−0.76) = +0.48 V; not suitable. In copper: E°cell = (−0.28) − (+0.34) = −0.62 V; suitable in the chapter's test.

90 [SOURCE, p.46; question 4-12] Q: Which metal among Al and Au can liberate H2 from H+ under the stated standard potentials?
A: Aluminum, because E°Al3+/Al = −1.66 V and E°H+/H2 = 0.00 V, giving +1.66 V for Al oxidation with H+ reduction.

91 [SOURCE, p.46] Q: Can copper metal deposit Zn from a standard Zn2+ solution? (E°Cu2+/Cu = +0.34, E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V.)
A: No; Cu + Zn2+ → Cu2+ + Zn gives E°cell = −0.76 − (+0.34) = −1.10 V.

92 [SOURCE, p.47; question 4-8] Q: Does 1 M HCl dissolve Ag spontaneously? E°Ag+/Ag = +0.80 V.
A: No. 2Ag + 2H+ → 2Ag+ + H2 gives E°cell = 0 − 0.80 = −0.80 V.

93 [SOURCE, pp.50–54] Q: Write line notation for Fe + Sn2+ → Fe2+ + Sn.
A: Fe(s) | Fe2+(1 M) || Sn2+(1 M) | Sn(s).

94 [SOURCE, pp.50–54] Q: Is Ag | Ag+ || Zn2+ | Zn a correctly oriented notation for a spontaneous standard cell (E°Ag+/Ag = +0.80, E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V)?
A: No. In the written direction E°cell = −0.76 − (+0.80) = −1.56 V; reverse the halves for a spontaneous cell.

95 [SOURCE, pp.50–55] Q: Can silver or copper be dissolved by hydrogen ions in the simple standard-potential test?
A: The file obtains E°cell = −0.80 V for Ag/H+ and −0.34 V for Cu/H+, so not spontaneously in the stated idealized model.

96 [SOURCE, p.52] Q: Can CuSO4 be stored in an aluminium cup?
A: No; Al + Cu2+ redox is spontaneous, E°cell = +2.00 V under the provided potentials.

97 [SOURCE, pp.50–52] Q: Can NaCl solution be stored in a silver or aluminium cup in the chapter's hypothetical standard test?
A: Ag gives E°cell = −3.50 V (0.80 vs −2.70); Al gives E°cell = −1.04 V (−1.66 vs −2.70): both proposed metal/Na+ displacement directions are nonspontaneous.

98 [SOURCE, pp.52–53] Q: Determine spontaneity of 2Cr + 3Zn2+ → 2Cr3+ + 3Zn, if E°Cr3+/Cr = −0.74 V and E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V.
A: E°cell = −0.76 − (−0.74) = −0.02 V: nonspontaneous in the written direction.

99 [SOURCE, p.54] Q: Why may an equation be written with anode and cathode opposite from the preferred pair based on potentials?
A: When the question specifies a reaction or a notation, determine oxidation/reduction from that direction first; the computed E°cell will reveal if the direction is nonspontaneous.

SECTION 4. ELECTROLYTIC CELLS, ELECTROPLATING, FARADAY LAWS [pp.59–96]
===========================================================

100 [SOURCE, pp.59–60] Q: Define an electrolytic cell.
A: A cell that uses electrical energy to drive a nonspontaneous chemical reaction; electrical energy is converted into chemical energy.

101 [SOURCE, pp.59–60; question 4-9] Q: Compare galvanic and electrolytic cells.
A: Galvanic: spontaneous, chemical → electrical, Ecell positive/ΔG negative for the reaction, e.g., Daniell cell. Electrolytic: externally driven, electrical → chemical, forward reaction nonspontaneous without power, e.g., electroplating. Oxidation remains at anode and reduction at cathode in both.

102 [SOURCE, pp.60–63] Q: Define electroplating and give its uses.
A: Depositing a thin layer of one metal onto another using electrolysis; used to coat surfaces and improve appearance or protection.

103 [SOURCE, pp.61–63] Q: What two factors does the chapter name as controlling electroplating quality?
A: Applied voltage/current conditions and concentration of the metal ions in the electroplating solution.

104 [SOURCE, pp.61–63] Q: At which electrode does plating occur?
A: The cathode, where dissolved metal ions gain electrons and deposit as metal.

105 [SOURCE, pp.61–63] Q: How is the plating metal replenished in a typical soluble-anode electroplating cell?
A: The metal anode dissolves by oxidation; metal ions deposit at the cathode.

106 [SOURCE, pp.64,82; question 4-2] Q: State Faraday's first law.
A: The mass deposited, dissolved, or released at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of charge that passes through the cell, for the same half-reaction.

107 [SOURCE, pp.64,84] Q: State Faraday's second law.
A: For an equal quantity of electrical charge, the masses of substances deposited or released are proportional to their chemical equivalent masses (molar mass divided by electrons per ion/molecule).

108 [SOURCE, pp.64,82; question 4-2] Q: Derive the approximate Faraday constant and its unit.
A: F = N_A × |charge of one electron| ≈ (6.023 × 10^23 mol−1)(1.6 × 10−19 C) ≈ 96,500 C per mole of electrons.

109 [SOURCE, pp.64–65] Q: Define electric current (I), electric charge (Q), and units.
A: I is current in amperes (A); Q is charge in coulombs (C). Q = It when time is measured in seconds.

110 [SOURCE, p.65] Q: Express the amount of electrons in moles from current and time.
A: n(e−) = It/F.

111 [SOURCE, pp.65–66] Q: How do you calculate the deposited mass from electrons passed?
A: n(metal) = It/(zF), then m = MIt/(zF), where z is electrons per atom/ion and M is molar mass.

112 [SOURCE, pp.65–66] Q: How do you convert deposited moles into number of atoms?
A: Number of atoms = n × N_A.

113 [SOURCE, pp.65–66] Q: How do you find gas amount during electrolysis under non-STP conditions?
A: PV = nRT using consistent units, then convert gas moles into electron moles from the balanced half-reaction.

114 [SOURCE, pp.65–66] Q: What gas relationship is used for electrolysis of water?
A: 2H2O → 2H2 + O2; hydrogen and oxygen gas have a 2:1 mole and volume ratio under the same temperature and pressure.

115 [SOURCE, pp.65–66] Q: How is electric charge allocated when two products compete at one electrode?

SECTION 5. STANDARD FREE ENERGY AND EQUILIBRIUM CONSTANT [pp.97–102, 120–127]
===========================================================

144 [SOURCE, pp.97–98] Q: What is the relation between Gibbs free energy and cell potential?
A: ΔG = −nFE and ΔG° = −nFE°. n is electrons transferred (in mole-equivalents) per stoichiometric reaction; F is Faraday constant.

145 [SOURCE, pp.97–98] Q: How is E°cell related to equilibrium constant?
A: ΔG° = −RT ln Keq = −nFE°cell; therefore E°cell = (RT/nF) ln Keq; at 25°C it is approximately (0.026/n) ln Keq.

146 [SOURCE, p.98] Q: What do E°cell, ΔG°, and Keq indicate when the cell reaction is spontaneous?
A: E°cell > 0, ΔG° < 0, Keq > 1 (for the forward reaction under the given standard thermodynamic relationship).

147 [SOURCE, p.98] Q: What if Keq = 1? Prove the relation.
A: ln(1) = 0; E°cell = (RT/nF) × 0 = 0; ΔG° = −nF × 0 = 0.

148 [SOURCE, p.98] Q: What if Keq < 1?
A: ln Keq < 0; E°cell < 0 and ΔG° > 0, with reactants favored at equilibrium.

149 [SOURCE, p.99; exercise 4-7] Q: Pb + 2Ag+ → Pb2+ + 2Ag. Given E°Ag+/Ag = +0.80 and E°Pb2+/Pb = −0.13, find E°cell, ΔG°, Keq and notation.

150 [SOURCE, p.100; exercise 4-9] Q: For 3Hg2^2+ + 2Cr → 6Hg + 2Cr3+, with E°Hg2^2+/Hg = +0.85 and E°Cr3+/Cr = −0.74, find ΔG°.
A: E°cell = +1.59 V; 6 mol electrons; ΔG° = −6 × 96500 × 1.59 = −920,610 J/mol.

SECTION 6. NERNST EQUATION / CONCENTRATION / pH [pp.102–132]

157 [SOURCE, pp.102–103; question 4-6] Q: Define the Nernst equation and write its general expression with symbols.
A: Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Qreaction. E = actual potential, E° = standard potential, R = gas constant, T = Kelvin, n = transferred electrons, F = Faraday constant, Qreaction = reaction quotient.

158 [SOURCE, p.103] Q: Write the Nernst equation at 25°C, as used in the chapter.
A: Ecell ≈ E°cell − (0.026/n) ln Qreaction.

159 [SOURCE, p.103] Q: When does Ecell = E°cell?
A: When Qreaction = 1, so ln Qreaction = 0; standard concentrations and pressure satisfy this in the simple cases presented.

160 [SOURCE, pp.103–104] Q: How is a reaction quotient constructed for Nernst problems?
A: Products over reactants, each raised to its balanced coefficient; activities of pure solids and pure liquids are 1, so they do not appear.

161 [SOURCE, p.104; example 4-8] Q: For 2Ag+ + Cu → 2Ag + Cu2+ at 25°C, [Ag+] = [Cu2+] = 0.01 M and E°Ag+/Ag = +0.80, E°Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V. Find E.

182 [SOURCE, pp.133–134] Q: Describe lead–acid (lead storage) battery construction and voltage.
A: Lead (Pb) anode, lead dioxide (PbO2) cathode, sulfuric acid electrolyte; a cell provides a little more than 2 V, with multiple cells giving common 6 V or 12 V arrangements.

183 [SOURCE, p.134] Q: Write the lead battery discharge anode half-reaction.

184 [SOURCE, p.134] Q: Write the lead battery discharge cathode half-reaction.

185 [SOURCE, p.134] Q: Write the lead battery total discharge reaction.
A: Pb + PbO2 + 2H2SO4 → 2PbSO4 + 2H2O.

186 [SOURCE, p.135] Q: Explain why sulfuric-acid density decreases during lead battery discharge.
A: Sulfuric acid is consumed and water forms; the sulfuric-acid solution becomes less concentrated/dense.

187 [SOURCE, pp.135–137] Q: Can a lead–acid battery be recharged? Explain.
A: Yes. An external current drives discharge reactions in reverse, converting PbSO4 back to Pb/PbO2 and restoring acid concentration.

188 [SOURCE, pp.135–138; question 4-19] Q: What are the anode and cathode of the dry cell made from, and what are its properties?

189 [SOURCE, pp.135–138; question 4-19] Q: Write the dry cell half-reactions and overall reaction as supplied in the chapter.
A: Anode: Zn + 2OH− → Zn(OH)2 + 2e−. Cathode: 2MnO2 + 2H2O + 2e− → 2MnO(OH) + 2OH−. Overall: Zn + 2MnO2 + 2H2O → Zn(OH)2 + 2MnO(OH). [These are the exact electrode-reaction style illustrated in the file.]

197 [SOURCE, pp.123–124] Q: In Al | Al3+(0.008 M) || H+(? M) | H2(1 atm) | Pt, the actual and standard cell potentials are equal. Find pH.
A: E = E° means Qreaction = 1. For 2Al + 6H+ → 2Al3+ + 3H2, Q = (0.008)^2/[H+]^6 = 1. [H+] = (0.008)^(1/3) = 0.20 M. pH = −log(0.20) ≈ 0.70. Line notation: Al | Al3+(0.008 M) || H+(0.20 M) | H2(1 atm) | Pt.

198 [SOURCE, p.124] Q: SHE/Zn cell with [H+] = 0.20 M, PH2 = 1 atm, [Zn2+] = 0.40 M, E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V. Find Ecell.
A: Q = 0.40/(0.20)^2 = 10. E°cell = 0.76 V. Ecell = 0.76 − 0.013 ln10 ≈ 0.730 V.

199 [SOURCE, p.124] Q: For 2Al + 3Cd2+ → 2Al3+ + 3Cd, with E°cell = 1.26 V and n = 6, find ΔG°.
A: ΔG° = −6 × 96500 × 1.26 = −729,540 J/mol.

200 [SOURCE, p.127] Q: For Ni + 2H+ → Ni2+ + H2 at 25°C, E°cell = 0.25 V, pH = 2, [Ni2+] = 0.001 M, PH2 = 1 atm. Find ΔG.
A: [H+] = 0.01 M; Q = 0.001/(0.01)^2 = 10. E = 0.25 − 0.013 ln10 ≈ 0.2201 V; ΔG ≈ −2 × 96500 × 0.2201 ≈ −42.48 kJ/mol (source rounds to around −42.46 kJ/mol).

201 [SOURCE, p.131] Q: For Ni + Sn2+(1 M) → Ni2+ + Sn, E°cell = 0.11 V and Ecell = 0.14 V at 25°C. Find [Ni2+].
A: 0.14 = 0.11 − 0.013 ln[Ni2+], hence ln[Ni2+] ≈ −2.308; [Ni2+] ≈ 0.10 M.

(ADDED PRACTICE BASED ON CHAPTER CONTENT — NOT CLAIMED AS MINISTERIAL)

P01 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: oxidation number]
Q: Find the oxidation number of Mn in KMnO4.
Answer: K = +1 and O = −2. 1 + x + 4(−2) = 0; x = +7.

P02 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: electron balancing]
Q: Balance the electron transfer in Zn + Ag+ → Zn2+ + Ag and identify oxidant/reductant.
Answer: Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−; 2Ag+ + 2e− → 2Ag. Overall Zn + 2Ag+ → Zn2+ + 2Ag. Reducing agent Zn; oxidizing agent Ag+.

P03 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: electrode selection / standard E]
Q: Given E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V and E°Ag+/Ag = +0.80 V, choose electrodes and calculate E°cell.
Answer: Zinc is anode; silver is cathode. E°cell = +0.80 − (−0.76) = +1.56 V.

P04 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: recover missing electrode potential]
Q: A Zn/Cu cell has E°cell = 1.10 V and E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V. Find E°Cu2+/Cu.
Answer: E°Cu2+/Cu = 1.10 + (−0.76) = +0.34 V.

P05 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: choose greatest voltage]
Q: From standard reductions Al3+/Al = −1.66, Zn2+/Zn = −0.76, Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V, choose a pair giving maximum E°cell.
Answer: Al anode, Cu cathode; E°cell = 0.34 − (−1.66) = +2.00 V.

P06 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: cell line notation]
Q: Write cell notation for Zn + 2Ag+ → Zn2+ + 2Ag under standard conditions.
Answer: Zn(s) | Zn2+(1 M) || Ag+(1 M) | Ag(s).

P07 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: spontaneous vessel/material reaction]
Q: Can copper metal spontaneously reduce Zn2+ to Zn if E°Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V and E°Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V?
Answer: E°cell for Cu + Zn2+ → Cu2+ + Zn = −0.76 − (+0.34) = −1.10 V; the proposed direction is nonspontaneous under standard conditions.

P08 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: Faraday charge and Cu mass]
Q: A 5.00 A current flows for 1930 s through CuSO4. Find the mass of Cu deposited. Use M_Cu = 63 g/mol and Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu.
Answer: Q = 5 × 1930 = 9650 C. n(e−) = 9650/96500 = 0.100 mol. n(Cu) = 0.0500 mol. Mass = 0.0500 × 63 = 3.15 g.

P09 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: current needed from desired mass]
Q: What current deposits 0.985 g Au from Au3+ solution in 965 s? M_Au = 197 g/mol.
Answer: n(Au) = 0.985/197 = 0.005 mol. n(e−) = 3 × 0.005 = 0.015 mol. I = 0.015 × 96500 / 965 = 1.50 A.

P10 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: time from molarity / remaining ions]
Q: 500 mL of 0.10 M Cu2+ solution is electrolyzed at 19.3 A. How long until only 0.010 mol Cu2+ remains?
Answer: Initially 0.500 × 0.10 = 0.050 mol; copper removed = 0.040 mol. Electrons required = 0.080 mol. t = 0.080 × 96500/19.3 = 400 s.

P11 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: atomic mass from Faraday]
Q: A monovalent metal deposits 0.540 g at 5 A for 193 s. Calculate molar mass.
Answer: n(e−) = (5 × 193)/96500 = 0.010 mol. n(metal) = 0.010 mol. M = 0.540/0.010 = 54 g/mol.

P12 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: number of atoms]
Q: A cathode deposits 0.020 mol Cu. How many atoms were deposited?
Answer: N = 0.020 × 6.02 × 10^23 = 1.204 × 10^22 atoms.

P13 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: gas volume at STP / electrons]
Q: How many electrons are needed to evolve 11.2 L oxygen at STP? Half-reaction: 2H2O → O2 + 4H+ + 4e−.
Answer: n(O2) = 11.2/22.4 = 0.50 mol. n(e−) = 4 × 0.50 = 2.00 mol electrons. Number = 1.204 × 10^24 electrons.

P14 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: PV = nRT for gas electrolysis]
Q: Oxygen gas occupies 0.244 L at 25°C and 1 atm. How many moles of electrons correspond to its formation? R = 0.08206 L atm mol−1 K−1.
Answer: n(O2) = 1 × 0.244/(0.08206 × 298) ≈ 0.0100 mol. Four electrons per O2: n(e−) ≈ 0.0400 mol.

P15 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: water electrolysis H2/O2 ratio]
Q: Water electrolysis produces a total 0.336 L of H2 + O2 at STP in 100 s. Find each gas volume and current.
Answer: H2 = (2/3) × 0.336 = 0.224 L; O2 = 0.112 L. n(O2) = 0.112/22.4 = 0.005 mol; n(e−) = 0.020 mol; I = 0.020 × 96500/100 = 19.3 A.

P16 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: current efficiency]
Q: 10 A flows for 965 s through Ag+ solution and only 80% of the charge deposits silver. M_Ag = 108 g/mol. Find deposited mass.
Answer: Total electron-moles = 10 × 965/96500 = 0.100 mol. Effective = 0.080 mol; each Ag+ needs 1e−; mass = 0.080 × 108 = 8.64 g.

P17 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: electrolysis cells in series]
Q: Two cells are in series. If 1.08 g Ag (M = 108, Ag+ needs 1 electron) deposits in one, how much Cu (M = 63, Cu2+ needs 2 electrons) deposits in the other?
Answer: Silver amount = 0.0100 mol, hence 0.0100 mol electrons travel through both cells. Cu deposited = 0.0100/2 = 0.0050 mol; mass = 0.005 × 63 = 0.315 g.

P18 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: competing cathode products and charge partition]
Q: 0.50 mol electrons are supplied in a copper electrolysis cell, with 2.24 L H2 evolved at STP. How much Cu deposits (M = 63)?
Answer: H2 = 2.24/22.4 = 0.10 mol, consuming 0.20 mol electrons. Left for Cu2+ = 0.30 mol electrons; Cu = 0.15 mol; mass = 9.45 g.

P19 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: standard Gibbs energy]
Q: A galvanic reaction transfers 2 electrons and has E°cell = +1.10 V. Calculate ΔG°.
Answer: ΔG° = −nFE° = −2 × 96500 × 1.10 = −212,300 J/mol = −212.3 kJ/mol.

P20 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: equilibrium constant from E°]
Q: At 25°C a cell with n = 2 has E°cell = 0.130 V. Find Keq using 0.026/n ln Keq.
Answer: ln Keq = 2 × 0.130/0.026 = 10; Keq = e^10 ≈ 2.20 × 10^4.

P21 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: actual cell potential from Nernst]
Q: Daniell cell: E° = 1.10 V, [Zn2+] = 0.20 M, [Cu2+] = 0.020 M, 25°C. Find E.
Answer: Qreaction = [Zn2+]/[Cu2+] = 10. E = 1.10 − (0.026/2) ln 10 ≈ 1.0701 V.

P22 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: unknown ionic concentration from E]
Q: For Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu, E° = 1.10 V, actual E = 1.0701 V, [Cu2+] = 0.10 M, 25°C. Find [Zn2+].
Answer: ln Q = (1.10 − 1.0701)/0.013 ≈ 2.30 → Q ≈ 10; [Zn2+] = 10 × 0.10 ≈ 1.0 M.

P23 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: hydrogen electrode potential from pH]
Q: Find the H+/H2 reduction potential at pH = 2, PH2 = 1 atm and 25°C.
Answer: [H+] = 10^−2 M; E = −(0.026/2) ln(1/[H+]²) = −0.013 ln(10^4) ≈ −0.1197 V.

P24 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: calculate pH from Nernst potential]
Q: For Ni + 2H+ → Ni2+ + H2 at 25°C, E° = 0.25 V, actual E ≈ 0.1901 V, [Ni2+] = 0.010 M, PH2 = 1 atm. Find pH.
Answer: E° − E = 0.0599 V. Thus lnQ ≈ 0.0599/0.013 ≈ 4.605; Q ≈ 100. Since Q = 0.010/[H+]², [H+]² = 10^−4, [H+] = 0.010 M, pH = 2.

P25 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: nonstandard free energy]
Q: In P21, E = 1.0701 V and n = 2. Calculate actual ΔG.
Answer: ΔG = −2 × 96500 × 1.0701 ≈ −206,529 J/mol = −206.5 kJ/mol.

P26 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: batteries connected in series]
Q: If a lead-acid battery uses 3 galvanic cells of approximately 2 V each in series, what approximate terminal voltage is obtained?
Answer: About 6 V. This is a voltage-in-series illustration, not a claim about how battery capacity/charge adds.

P27 [WORKED PRACTICE — idea: battery half-reaction stoichiometry]
Q: In lead battery discharge Pb + PbO2 + 2H2SO4 → 2PbSO4 + 2H2O, how many moles H2SO4 are consumed for 0.50 mol Pb reacting (assuming complete reaction)?
Answer: 2 mol H2SO4 per 1 mol Pb; for 0.50 mol Pb, consume 1.00 mol H2SO4.

SECTION 10. SOURCE QUALITY / ACCURACY WARNINGS
===========================================================

1. No official ministry-year labels were legible for these exercise IDs. Do not attach made-up ministry years to the questions; to verify genuine ministry provenance, a year-tagged official exam source is needed.
2. The PDF repeatedly calls the galvanic anode 'positive' and galvanic cathode 'negative' (pp.18,21,30). That is incorrect for the usual DISCHARGING galvanic cell: its anode is negative and cathode positive. The universal accurate rule is oxidation at anode and reduction at cathode. For an electrolytic cell driven by an external supply, the anode is positive and cathode negative.
3. Ni2+/Ni appears as both −0.24 V and −0.25 V (and at least one reproduced question appears to omit a negative sign). Select the potential explicitly supplied by each original problem; this changes E° answers by ~0.01 V.
4. Some molar masses/species in the repeated series-cell exercise around p.78 are inconsistent (the example refers to AgNO3/CaCl2 but subsequently uses Cu; and 'Ag = 180 g/mol' is printed in one place). Preserve the correct species from the original question before calculating a numeric key. The worked practice problem P17 is internally consistent and is separately marked as new practice.
5. Several Faraday examples round electron-moles too early. For example p.67 uses 0.9 mol electrons for 25 A × 1 h although the unrounded value with F = 96,500 is 0.9326 mol. Both the teacher-key rounded result and the accurate result are identified where helpful.
6. In the Nernst/ΔG worked examples, some intermediate rounding causes ~0.001 V variations (notably p.132 Cd/Cu). Use the stated 0.026/n approximation consistently, or the full RT/nF expression, and state your convention.
7. Garbled/rearranged characters occur in PDF extraction, especially Arabic overlay, mathematical superscripts, and a few printed problem statements. I have not manufactured missing numbers, missing questions, or years. When a literal source expression appeared inconsistent, the question was identified or the calculation was explained from readable data.
8. This file is a consolidated study deck, not a certified verbatim transcript of every sentence. Exact page excerpts remain in the original PDF. Printed questions have been grouped by idea; repeated examples with distinct data have generally been kept.
